I look like I have been waiting in a queue for three hours. **She:** You look like you. That is all I ever wanted. Then she sent her own photo. It was a passport photo too — taken at the same kind of machine, the same pale background, the same fluorescent honesty. She wore a simple hijab, her face soft and open, her eyes carrying the exact warmth he had heard in her voice a thousand times. **She:** Now you have me too. He saved it immediately. He did not know how to describe the feeling. It was not desire, not exactly. It was recognition. It was the strange, holy relief of finding that the person you love exists, actually exists, in the world, with a face that can be looked at and remembered. **He:** I will carry this in my pocket everywhere I go. **She:** It will get ruined. **He:** That is fine. I will take another one. I will take a thousand photos of you. I will fill a whole room. **She:** You are ridiculous. **He:** I am in love. It is the same thing.

The visa was harder. They discovered this together, side by side through the glowing rectangle of their screens. He needed an invitation letter from a Malaysian citizen, a sponsor with an address and a declaration of responsibility. She was sixteen, still in school, living with her parents. But she had an aunt, the one whose tea plantations she had photographed — a woman with a house in the highlands who had heard enough about Leon through Nazurah's careful, glowing descriptions to say yes. The letter took three drafts. Nazurah wrote it herself on her aunt's behalf, her words precise and formal, the way she wrote her school essays — only this was more important than any essay. *I, the undersigned, declare that I am the sponsor and host of Mr. Leon --- of Kampala, Uganda, for the duration of his visit to Malaysia. He is a dear friend of my family. His safety and conduct are my responsibility while he is in the country.* She read it aloud to him over a call, her voice steady until the last line, where it faltered. **She:** I have not told my mother yet. The other letter. The real one. The one I will have to get her to sign. He was quiet. Then he said the hardest thing he had said in weeks. **He:** You should not lie to her. **She:** I know. **He:** If she signs it, I want her to sign it knowing who I am. She might say no. **She:** She might. **He:** Then we will find another way. But we will not build the door on a false foundation. She was silent. Then she said, softly, **She:** You are right. You are always right. That is infuriating. **He:** I am not always right. But I am right about this. The next evening, she told her mother everything. Not all at once — a cup of tea, a kitchen table, the rain starting outside. She told her about the boy in Uganda who wrote songs and said her name like a prayer. She told her about the long nights, the voice notes, the rain she had recorded, the kitchen in her dream. She told her about the invitation letter waiting on the table, and the plan — the long, fragile, improbable plan — to bring him here. Her mother listened. Set down her cup. And said, **She:** How do you know he is who he says he is? Nazurah had no answer that satisfied her. So she gave the only one she had. **She:** I have heard his heartbeat. He held the phone to his chest once, at night, so I could hear it. And I heard it, Mother. It was real. It is real. Her mother looked at her for a long time. Then she picked up the pen and signed. She did not say yes. She did not say she approved. She said one thing, her voice tired and full of care: **Mother:** If he hurts my daughter, the ocean will not protect him. Nazurah laughed — a bright, helpless sound — and kissed her mother's cheek. **She:** He will not. He is too good. That is why I love him.

The application was submitted on a Thursday in the middle of March. They filled out the online form together, Leon's finger hovering over the keyboard while she read his answers back to him, correcting his grammar, laughing at his mistakes, making him laugh so hard he had to step away from the computer. When the payment went through, they both fell quiet. **She:** It is done. **He:** It is done. It felt like a doorway, too small to see through, but real — a handle turning, a hinge moving somewhere in the dark. He sent her a voice note that night. She expected the usual — the hum of his room, the distant dogs, his voice low and warm. Instead, there was music. A rough guitar, out of tune in places, played on a borrowed instrument. And then his voice, singing. The melody was simple, almost childlike — the kind of tune that sticks because it has no reason not to. The words were about rain sent across an ocean, a kitchen in a dream, a door that opens inward toward someone waiting. He called it "The Open Door." He did not sing it well. He forgot a line, laughed, started again. The recording was imperfect and raw and entirely his. She played it in the dark, three times, four, five — until she had memorized every stumble, every breath, every note. Then she replied with her voice shaking. **She:** That is the most beautiful thing anyone has ever made for me. **He:** It is not finished. **She:** It is. It is more than finished. It is mine. He went quiet. When he spoke again, there was something wet and unsteady in his voice. **He:** I want to sing it to you in person. In the airport. I want to take your face in my hands and sing it to you so you can hear it without the phone between us. **She:** Then you will have to practice. You missed a line. **He:** I was nervous. **She:** You were singing to me. Why would you be nervous? **He:** Because you are the only audience I will ever care about. And the only one I have ever been afraid of. **She:** Good. Keep that fear. It will keep you humble. **He:** I am always humble. **She:** You are not. But I love you anyway. **He:** That is good. Because I also love me anyway. **She:** Leon. **He:** Yes. **She:** I am going to love you for a very long time. **He:** I am counting on it. I am building my whole life on it.

The last week of March moved slowly, the way all waiting moves — each day a full stop, each night a comma. He checked the visa status every morning, standing at his window with the phone warm in his hand, watching the mango tree beyond the yard. She checked it every night, curled in her chair, the same three words glowing back at her: *In process. In process. In process.* On the last night of the month, he called her just before midnight. Neither of them spoke for a while. They just breathed, the way they had in the beginning, two people learning to exist in the same silence. Then he said, **He:** In three hours it will be April. **She:** I know. **He:** Every month I have known you has been a season of rain and song. I do not know what April will bring. But I know I will be bringing it to you. **She:** You cannot bring a month across the ocean. **He:** I can try. I am trying. And then, because he could not help himself, because he never could: **He:** One day I will bring you all the Aprils. All the mangoes. All the rains. Every song I have not written yet. I will lay them at your feet and call it a life. She felt the tears coming and did not stop them. **She:** I will be there. I will gather them up. And I will say Salam — she said it softly, the word now heavier and more precious than it had ever been — **She:** Salam, my love. Across the ocean, in the dark of his room, he smiled. **He:** Salam, my heart. I am on my way. The clock turned. April began. And the door, still open, began to widen.
